One task, one goal

Each conversation is a task: one thing you want done, like “tighten chapter 3” or “add a section on limits”. The assistant starts by saying the goal back to you in a sentence. When it thinks the goal is met, it ends its reply with Is this task done? Yes, it’s done saves your open files, keeps a named version in History (“Task done: …”), and starts a fresh task. The old one stays in the Tasks view, under Done, and you can go back to it.

Keeping each conversation to one job keeps the assistant on it. A long conversation that wanders from chapter to chapter gets slower, and loses track of what you asked last. Done and New task sit just above the message box. ⌘T starts a new task and ⇧⌘D marks this one done, whenever you like.

Several tasks can run at once. Start a new one while the assistant is working and the first carries on in the background. A notice tells you when it’s finished or needs you.

The Tasks view

Every task in the book is in the Tasks view, down the left of the window, with an amber badge when one needs you. They’re grouped as Needs you (a question for you, a permission to give, or a reply you haven’t read), At work, Open and Done, by day and then by month. Each says what it’s doing (“Reading ‘The Carew Murder Case’ (chapter 4)”), or the mode it ran in and the chapters it changed.

Click a task and it opens in the assistant pane, and what it changed folds out beneath it, as a version does in History. Search them, or show only those that touched This chapter. Open tasks that haven’t moved in two weeks can be marked done together. The menu at the top of the assistant pane is for switching: find a task by name, or go to one that needs you.

You can keep typing while it works. ⏎ holds your message until it finishes. ⌥⏎ sends it now, and the assistant reads it at its next step (with assistants that allow this, like Claude Code). Messages waiting to go sit above the box, where you can edit them or take them back.

It tells you what it did

As it works, the assistant’s pane fills with what it’s doing, a line per step, in words about your book. Chapters are called by their titles and numbered the way your book numbers them.

  • Read “Story of the Door” (chapter 1) · lines 1–30
  • Searched for “lighthouse” in chapters/ · 3 matches
  • Ran the Python script plot.py
  • Asked you a question · you said “Warm”
  • Changed Foreword’s settings · text size 15 px, rulers at 100

Each step has a coloured dot: blue for reading, violet for changing, grey for thinking, amber when it needs you, red when something went wrong. A change says where it was and how many words it touched (“line 6 +2 −7 words”). Show change opens it word by word, with the words it cut struck through and the words it wrote in their place. From there, Show in editor takes you to it, and Undo this edit takes it out (and ⌘Z in the editor puts it back).

When a reply points to a place in the book, like chapters/one.typ:42 or “one.typ, lines 42–48”, it’s a link: a click puts the cursor on that line in the editor, or selects the lines. Only the book’s own files are links, so “10:30” never is.

Under the reply is how long it took, and Saved in History, which opens the version History kept. Below the conversation, Changed in this task lists each file it has touched so far.

Many requests start from a button rather than from you typing: Write it, Fix This Error, Tighten, Fix all. A button sends the assistant a full set of instructions, but the chat shows only what you asked for, in a line (“Revise this passage · Tighten it”), so a one-click request doesn’t look like a page you wrote.

The lines it changed stay marked

Every line the assistant wrote gets a violet bar beside the line number and a faint tint. The mark stays until you rewrite the line, and then it’s yours. Rest the pointer on the line number to see which request changed it, when, and what the line said before. Lines it read in this conversation get a thin blue bar. You can choose both colours in Settings ▸ Editor ▸ The assistant’s marks.

The marks come from History, so they cover every change the assistant made, however it made it. They’re still there after you quit and come back, and they move with the text as you write above them.

You’ll notice the assistant writes one sentence per line. Typst joins the lines of a paragraph, so the printed book looks exactly the same, and each mark, change and comment covers a sentence rather than a whole paragraph.

It works the other way too. If you rewrite a sentence the assistant wrote, your next message tells it what you changed. Otherwise, asked to look at that passage again, it would remember its own version, and could quietly put it back.

A bar under what you select

Select a passage, in your text or on the typeset pages, and a bar comes up under it, each button in the colour of what it does.

Revise
Changes the passage in place. Say how, or pick one of the usual requests: Tighten, Proofread, Clearer, Simpler words, Expand, and Match my style guide when the book has one.
Options
Three versions to pick from, with Shorter, Plainer and More vivid a click away.
Ask
A question about the passage, in your own words, or one of the usual ones: Explain it, Is it clear?, Check the facts, What’s weakest?, Does it fit?, and Against my style guide when the book has one. The answer comes in the chat, and the passage stays as it is.
Comment
Leaves a comment on it, to come back to.
Add to chat
Takes the passage to the chat, with the cursor in the message box, for anything else (⌘I).

Over a Typst error, Fix error comes first. The bar sits under the end of the selection, so it never covers what you’re reading, and in a narrow editor all but the first button show just their icons.

A note under the passage follows a Revise. While the assistant works it says Revising…, with Stop, and the passage is marked in violet. Then it says how much changed (“Revised −11 words”), with Undo, which puts the passage back in one step, and Show change, the words it changed. After an Ask, the note says Answering… until the answer is in the chat. Revise switches the chat to Write, Options to Options, and Ask to Ask, so a question never changes your text. Add to chat leaves the mode as it is. If a reply is still running, the request waits for it, with Send now and Cancel.

Pointing at a passage in the chat

Whatever you’ve selected goes with your next message. It shows above the box as a quote, named by its place in the book (“Story of the Door · lines 5–8”). ⌘I pins it, so it stays while you select something else. You can select on the typeset pages too, and Foreword finds the same words in your text.

Formulas, as your book sets them

When the assistant writes a formula or a snippet of Typst in the chat, the chat shows it typeset, using your book’s own definitions. A formula in your book’s notation looks the way it will print, and a box your book defines, like a key-idea box or an exercise, looks like that box. Review comments, What changed and Options show them the same way.

Five modes

The mode button on the message box decides what kind of help you get. ⇧Tab moves from one to the next. Each mode keeps its own assistant, model and effort, so you can discuss with one and write with another.

Write
Writes and edits the book.
Ask
Answers questions about the book, in the chat, and changes nothing. After an answer, Go ahead switches to Write and makes the change you talked about.
Discuss
Thinks a direction through with you: two or three real options, what each costs, and which it would pick. It argues against a weaker idea, and points to your own lines. It changes nothing until you press Write it, and then it says what you agreed before it writes.
Options
Three versions of the selected passage to choose from. You put one in.
Figures
Diagrams, charts, illustrations and tables, drawn in Typst, so they appear in the preview.

In Ask, Discuss and Options, “changes nothing” is enforced by Foreword, not left to the assistant’s good behaviour: any attempt to change a file is refused.

When another mode would suit

Ask “should the exercises stay in their own chapter at the end, or should each chapter end with its own?” in Write, and there’s nothing to write yet. It’s a question of direction. So the assistant says so, and under its reply asks How should it go on?, offering two ways forward in a sentence each: Discuss (“Weigh a single closing exercises chapter against exercises at the end of each chapter, and recommend one. Nothing changes.”) or Write (“Move the exercises to the end of each chapter, splitting chapter VII up.”). One click picks. It offers this at most once a reply, and never offers again a mode you’ve turned down in this task.

Three versions: Options

Select a passage and say what should change: “make it punchier”, “less formal”. You get three complete versions side by side, each with a short label and a line on how it differs. Use this puts one in place, like any edit you’d make, so ⌘Z takes it out. The others then say Use this instead, so you can swap.

The versions stay with their passage. Use this puts one where the passage is now, even if you’ve since moved the cursor, selected other words, or written above it. A follow-up such as “shorter”, with nothing selected, is about the same passage. If you’ve changed the passage itself since you asked, it won’t write over your changes. The card shows what you changed, and offers New options for it as it is, Replace mine anyway, or Cancel.

Two tabs switch how the versions look. Text shows them as you’d write them. Preview sets each as it would print, with italics, bold and maths, so you compare three passages rather than three lots of markup. Foreword remembers which you used last.

When it needs a decision

Rather than burying questions in a long reply, the assistant asks them in a panel that takes the message box’s place, one at a time. Each has a short title, two to four answers with a line each, and the one it would pick marked Recommended. There’s always room for an answer of your own. Number keys choose, the arrow keys move between questions, and a last tab shows all your answers before you send them. Whatever you’d typed in the message box is still there afterwards.

Slash commands

Type / in the message box for a list of shortcuts. “Rewrite this /options” switches to Options and leaves “Rewrite this” in the box, ready to send. A slash inside a word, as in “and/or”, is left alone.

Modes
/write /ask /discuss /options /figures
Tasks
/new /done /stop /resume
Settings
/model sonnet /effort high /assistant codex
The book
/fix (every open review comment) /export /pdf

Ask it to change Foreword

“Make the text bigger.” “Put a ruler at 80.” “Switch me to Claude Code.” The assistant can change Foreword’s settings, so these just happen, and its account says what changed: “Changed Foreword’s settings · text size 15 px, rulers at 80”. Unless you’ve said otherwise, it asks you first. A change to the assistant itself (its model, its mode, which assistant it is) waits until its reply is finished.

It can’t change its own permissions. Those are yours, in Settings, so nothing it reads in a book can talk it into more.

What it doesn’t do

  • How well it follows the style guide, offers another mode, or asks before it guesses depends on the model. A stronger model does each more reliably.
  • With Codex, the account says a file was edited but can’t show the words. History has the full change.
  • A formula in the chat is drawn on its own, outside the book, so a reference to another part of the book shows as its label, in grey.
  • Marks show in the files you have open in tabs.
  • Which tasks are done is kept on your Mac, not in the book.
