FeaturesWriting

The editor

Where you write. A Typst book is plain text with a little markup, and the editor shows it close to how it will print: headings in bold, emphasis in italic. Around it are the things a long book needs: search across every chapter, a table of contents to move by, and chapters you can find by number. If you’ve used VS Code, it’s the same editor, with the same keys.

Writing in it

Headings are bold, maths is coloured, _emphasis_ is in italic and *strong* in bold, as they’ll print. A note to yourself, like // TODO: check this date, gets a coloured tag, and a mark in the scrollbar so you can find it again. FIXME and NOTE work the same way. Rest the pointer on a TODO, or put the cursor on its line, and a small bar offers Ask the assistant to do it, Done, which takes the note out, and All 12 TODOs, the To do list.

Select a word and type _, and the word is wrapped, _word_, and still selected, so typing * makes it bold as well. The same works for *, $, brackets and quotes. ⌘Z undoes a word or so at a time, and ⇧⌘Z redoes. A change Foreword makes for you, like taking out a TODO, is a step of its own.

A misspelling gets a red squiggle and a grammar slip a blue one, as in Word, checked on your Mac. See Spelling and grammar.

Long lines wrap at the edge of the pane, at a column you choose, or not at all, with guide lines wherever you want them (Settings ▸ Editor ▸ Long lines). The text size is separate from the rest of the window.

Tidied when you save

⌘S tidies the markup first: the spacing in code, the columns of multi-line equations lined up, one blank line around each heading, and no runs of blank lines. None of it changes the printed page, and your sentences and where you broke your lines stay as you wrote them. ⌘Z takes out just the tidying. It tidies the way VS Code’s Typst extension does, so a co-author working there won’t see your files change. It’s on to start with; turn it off in Settings ▸ Editor.

Finding your way

Search the book

⇧⌘F searches every chapter at once, including what you haven’t saved yet. Results are grouped under each chapter’s number and title, in reading order. Replace All asks first, then saves a version in History before it changes anything, so one click takes the whole book back.

Contents

The book’s chapters in order, numbered as the book numbers them, each with its sections and roughly how many words it has, and the whole book’s count at the top. The section you’re in is lit, and Contents opens scrolled to it, so in chapter 17 of a long book you don’t have to hunt for your place. Switch to another chapter and it follows. Click any heading to go there. Collapse all folds it down to just the chapters.

⌘-click to go there

As in VS Code, hold ⌘ over a name your book defines, like #key-idea, and it’s underlined as a link, with its definition and the comments above it under the pointer. ⌘-click opens its file at the definition. A reference works the same way: hold ⌘ over @ch:vectors to see the heading, figure or table it points to, and ⌘-click to open that chapter there, whether the preview shows the whole book or just one chapter. A citation opens your bibliography at its entry. A file’s name in quotes works the same way: #include "chapters/one.typ" opens the chapter, and image("figures/map.png") opens the picture in Preview. Typst’s own functions, like #figure, are part of Typst, so there’s nowhere to go.

Chapters by number

Go to File (⌘P) lists chapters as “4. Simplest Cases”, so typing 4 finds chapter 4, whatever its file is called. The numbers are the book’s own: a preface or prologue without a number doesn’t throw the count off. Contents, Search and the assistant use the same numbers.

Keys and commands

The command palette (⇧⌘P) lists Foreword’s commands, each with its shortcut, your recent ones first, so you can find a command by typing a word of it. Tabs work as they do in VS Code: ⌘W to close, ⇧⌘T to reopen, ⌃⇥ to step through. Under the tabs, a bar shows where you are in the chapter, heading by heading.

A colour for each book

With two books open, every window looks the same. Give each book a colour (Settings ▸ This book ▸ Colour, or Surprise me) and its title bar, the icons down the side, and the status bar take it on. If you’ve already given the book a colour in VS Code with the Peacock extension, Foreword uses the same one.

Light or dark

Settings ▸ General ▸ Appearance ▸ Theme is As the Mac is (to start with), Light or Dark. It changes the whole window: the editor, the chat and the window’s own buttons.

For someone new

The first time you open a book, a short tour shows you around the window, one part at a time, nine steps in about a minute. Help ▸ Take the Tour runs it again. Typst basics, from the welcome screen, opens beside your book and takes about five minutes. Typst is far smaller than LaTeX: headings are =, emphasis is _, and most of the rest the assistant can write for you.

What it doesn’t do

  • No suggestions as you type Typst’s commands.
  • Search doesn’t look inside pictures, PDFs or exports.
  • Word counts leave out code, maths and labels, so they’re approximate.
